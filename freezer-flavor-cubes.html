Freezer flavour cubes

Portion concentrated flavour into an ice cube tray and one cube seasons one block of tofu. Made once, it removes the seasoning decision from every weeknight for months.

Why this works so well with tofu specifically

Tofu contributes texture and protein and almost nothing else. Flavour is entirely your job, and the thing that usually stops people cooking it on a Tuesday is not the tofu, it is the ten minutes of deciding what to put on it and whether the fridge contains the four things that decision requires.

A tray of frozen flavour bases removes that step. Each cube holds roughly two tablespoons of concentrated paste, which is close to the right amount for one block. You take one out, and the seasoning question is already answered.

It also solves a problem specific to tofu. Thin liquid marinades run off a dense block and deliver very little, as the seasoning guide explains. A thick paste clings to the surface and browns into a crust, and thick pastes are exactly what freeze well in a tray.

The third benefit is waste. Half a jar of curry paste, a bunch of herbs about to turn, the end of a tub of miso: these are the things that die in the back of a refrigerator. Frozen in a tray they last months instead of days.

What to freeze in

A standard hard plastic ice cube tray works and most people already own one. Silicone trays are worth the small upgrade, because frozen paste releases from silicone with a push from underneath and has to be chiselled out of hard plastic. A lid keeps the cubes from taking on freezer smells during the first few hours, which matters more with garlic and herbs than you would expect.

Cube size decides your portion. Standard trays give about one tablespoon per cube, larger trays about two. Two tablespoons is the useful unit for one block of tofu, so with a standard tray plan on two cubes rather than one.

Once frozen solid, pop every cube out and move them into labelled freezer bags. Leaving them in the tray ties up the tray and exposes the cubes to air, and frozen pastes all look identical after a month.

What this needs:
  • A silicone ice cube tray with a lid, the only purchase this system requires
  • An immersion blender, useful for herb and nut bases in small quantities
  • Containers for the cubes once they leave the tray

Twelve is a starting list, not a limit. The rule is that a base needs salt, fat and something aromatic, concentrated enough that two tablespoons can carry a whole block.

What freezes well and what does not

Freezes cleanly: oil-based pastes, nut and seed pastes, fermented pastes like miso and gochujang, tomato concentrates, herb purees with oil, roasted garlic, ginger and aromatics blitzed with oil.

Freezes acceptably: coconut milk bases, which separate on thawing and come back together when whisked or warmed. Dairy-based sauces, which do the same but less reliably.

Does not freeze well: anything thickened with cornstarch or flour, which turns gluey. Fresh dairy on its own. Raw garlic in large quantity, which grows harsh over a couple of months. Anything mostly water, which just gives you a flavoured ice cube that dilutes the pan.

Herbs are worth a note. Blitzed with oil they hold their colour and taste for months. Frozen dry they go black and taste of nothing. The oil is doing real work, not just carrying flavour.

A batch session

Four bases in about an hour, and the result lasts a season.

  1. Pick four bases that share ingredients so nothing is bought for a single use. Garlic and oil appear in most of them.
  2. Make each one thick. Thicker than you would serve it, since you will be loosening it later with pasta water, lime juice or coconut milk.
  3. Taste for salt at paste strength, not at sauce strength. It should read as slightly too salty on a spoon, because it is about to be diluted across a whole block of tofu.
  4. Fill the tray, cover, and freeze solid. Overnight is safest.
  5. Pop out, bag, label with the name and the month, and return to the freezer.

Use within about three months for herb and nut bases. Fermented and tomato bases hold considerably longer without noticeable loss.

Using a cube

For crisped tofu, thaw the cube, toss it with the pressed cubes before cooking if the base is oil-heavy, or add it to the hot pan off the heat at the end if it contains sugar or fermented paste. Sugar burns fast, which is why gochujang and miso go in last. The crisping guide covers the timing.

For a blended sauce, drop the thawed cube straight into a blender with a block of silken tofu. That is exactly what tofu pesto sauce does, and every base in the table above works the same way.

For roasting, toss the thawed cube through cut tofu and vegetables before they go on the sheet pan. See the oven guide for temperatures.

Thawing takes about fifteen minutes at room temperature for a two tablespoon cube, or thirty seconds in a microwave in short bursts with a stir between each. Do not thaw a cube in a hot pan and expect it to behave. Oil-based pastes split when they hit high heat straight from frozen, and the water coming off the cube will steam whatever is already in the pan. Thaw first, then cook.

Skip this system if: you cook tofu once a month or you enjoy deciding what to make each time. A tray of frozen pastes is a solution to repetition, and if you are not repeating, it is twelve small blocks of freezer clutter. It also does nothing for texture. A cube on an unpressed block still produces wet tofu, and pressing remains the step that matters most.

Beyond tofu

Nothing about this is tofu-specific. The same cubes season roasted vegetables, stir into soup, thin into salad dressing, spread on toast, or go straight into a pan of rice. Tofu is simply the ingredient that benefits most, because it starts from zero and takes whatever you give it.
