What is actually in tofu

Protein, calcium, fat and calories vary more between blocks than most people expect, and two of the reasons are printed on the package.

Two things drive every number

Tofu is soybeans, water and a coagulant. How much water was pressed out and which coagulant was used explain almost all the variation you will see between packages, and both are why figures quoted online so often disagree.

Water content. Firmness is a measure of how much water was removed. Since the remaining protein and fat are concentrated into less weight, a firmer block carries more protein per hundred grams than a softer one. Nothing was added. There is simply less water diluting it.

The coagulant. Calcium sulfate contributes a substantial amount of calcium. Magnesium chloride, often labelled nigari, contributes very little. Two packages with near-identical ingredient lists can differ several-fold on the calcium line for this reason alone.

The practical consequence: the label on the block in your hand is more reliable than any table, including the one below.

Some reference figures

The United States Department of Agriculture's FoodData Central database holds separate entries for tofu set with different coagulants, and the gap between them illustrates the point better than any explanation.

Per 100g, rawFirm, calcium sulfate setFirm, calcium sulfate and nigari
Caloriesabout 144about 78
Proteinabout 17.3 gabout 9.0 g
Fatabout 8.7 gabout 5 g
Carbohydrateabout 2.8 gabout 2 g
Calciumabout 683 mgabout 201 mg

Both entries are labelled firm. The difference between them is larger than the difference most people imagine exists between tofu and other proteins, and it comes down to water content and coagulant rather than to anything the manufacturer added.

Commercial blocks in a supermarket sit across this whole range and beyond it. Silken tofu is considerably lower in protein per hundred grams because it is mostly water. Super-firm vacuum-packed tofu is at the top end because it has been pressed hardest.

Protein quality

Soy is one of the few plant foods that contains all nine essential amino acids in useful quantities, which is what people mean by a complete protein. That is a genuine point in its favour and it is also frequently overstated, since a varied diet supplies the full range regardless of whether any single food does.

What matters more practically is protein per serving, and that is a firmness question. A block of super-firm tofu delivers considerably more than the same weight of silken.

Cooking does not destroy the protein. Pressing removes water rather than nutrients, so a pressed block is slightly smaller and slightly more concentrated, which is the same relationship as everything else on this page.

Calcium and iron

Calcium-set tofu is one of the more substantial plant sources of calcium available, and this is entirely down to the coagulant rather than to the soybean. If calcium is a reason you are eating tofu, read the ingredient list for calcium sulfate and check the nutrition panel, because a nigari-set block will not do the same job.

Tofu contains a useful amount of iron, in the non-haem form found in plant foods, which the body absorbs less efficiently than the iron in meat. Vitamin C in the same meal improves that absorption, which is a reasonable argument for the lime, lemon or vinegar that most recipes here already include for flavour reasons.

Tofu contains no vitamin B12 unless a manufacturer has fortified it.

How cooking changes the numbers

Pressing removes water, so the same block weighs less afterward and every figure per hundred grams rises slightly. Nothing has been gained. The weight has just gone down.

Oil is the real change. Two to three tablespoons in a pan for one block adds a substantial amount of fat and calories to the finished dish, although well-pressed tofu absorbs far less of it than people assume and most stays in the pan. Air frying uses a small fraction of that, which is the clearest nutritional argument for the air fryer method.

Starch coatings add a small amount of carbohydrate. Sauces are usually where the sugar and sodium enter, and a heavy barbecue or sweet glaze contributes more of both than the tofu contributes of anything.

Freezing changes texture and not composition. A frozen and thawed block has the same nutrients in a chewier arrangement, as the freezing guide explains.

Reading a package properly

Three lines are worth checking before anything else. The firmness word tells you roughly where on the range the block sits. The ingredient list tells you which coagulant was used, and therefore what the calcium figure will look like. The net weight tells you whether a recipe written for sixteen ounces will be under-seasoned with the twelve ounce block in your hand.

Ingredient lists on plain tofu are usually three items long. Anything with a longer list is a flavoured or marinated product, which is a different food with different numbers, usually more sodium.

The types guide covers what the firmness labels mean in practice and which block suits which dish.

A note on precision: the figures on this page are reference values from a public database, not measurements of the block you bought. Brands vary, batches vary, and databases disagree with each other. For anything where the exact number matters to you, use the panel on the package and weigh the portion, and take any nutrition question specific to your own health to a doctor or a registered dietitian rather than to a cooking site.
