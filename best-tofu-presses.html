Tofu presses

Three designs, what actually separates them, and an honest account of who should not buy one at all.

The honest starting position

A tofu press is a convenience purchase, not a requirement. A clean towel and a heavy skillet remove a comparable amount of water for nothing, and the finished tofu is not dramatically different. Anyone telling you a press is essential is selling one.

What a press actually buys you is even pressure across the whole face of the block, a contained mess, no towels to wash, and the ability to load it and walk away for an hour without checking on it. Whether those things are worth twenty to thirty dollars depends entirely on how often you cook tofu.

The rough test: if you press tofu less than twice a month, use a skillet. If you press it weekly, a press pays for itself in irritation avoided. The direct comparison covers what each method actually delivers.

The three designs

Screw presses

Two plates held by threaded posts with knobs at the top. You tighten the knobs by hand, and you keep tightening every few minutes as the block compresses. These apply the most force of any design and produce the driest tofu.

The trade is attention. A screw press that you set and forget applies almost no pressure after the first few minutes, because the block has shrunk away from the plates. Used properly it wants three or four visits over twenty minutes.

They also need somewhere for the water to go, which usually means sitting on a plate or in the sink. Some models include a tray, many do not.

Spring presses

Plates held apart by springs, which compress as the tofu gives way and keep applying steady moderate pressure without any intervention. Load it, put it in the refrigerator, come back later.

Less total force than a screw press, so slightly wetter tofu, but the pressure is continuous rather than declining. For most cooking the difference in the finished result is small.

Spring presses are the easiest to live with, which in practice matters more than peak performance.

Container presses

A box with an inner drainer and a lid, often using a silicone band or a screw lid to apply pressure. Everything is contained, the drained water collects in the base, and the same container doubles as a marinating and storage vessel afterward.

These are the tidiest by a wide margin and the best choice if counter space and mess are your objections. Pressure is moderate, similar to a spring press.

The single best predictor of whether a press was a good purchase is whether it goes in the dishwasher. Every other feature is downstream of the thing actually being used.

How much water should come out

A sixteen ounce block of extra-firm tofu will typically release two to four tablespoons under moderate pressure over twenty to thirty minutes. Firm tofu gives more. A well-pressed block loses roughly a fifth of its thickness and feels dense and slightly springy rather than yielding.

Past that point the returns fall away quickly, and very heavily pressed tofu turns dry and dense in the middle rather than tender. This is why maximum force is a weaker selling point than it sounds. The pressing guide covers how to tell when a block is actually done.

Do not apply maximum pressure immediately to soft or medium tofu. Too much force too fast splits the block instead of draining it. Start light and increase after ten minutes.

Skip a press entirely if: you own a heavy skillet, you cook tofu occasionally rather than weekly, or you mostly buy super-firm vacuum-packed tofu, which has already been pressed at the factory and needs nothing but a pat with a towel. Buying a press before you have a working method is how a cupboard fills with things nobody uses. Cook tofu four or five times with a skillet on a towel first, and let the annoyance decide whether you want the upgrade.

What not to buy

Marinade injectors, vacuum marinating containers and tofu-specific cubing gadgets all address problems that pressing and freezing solve better and cheaper. The reason marinade does not penetrate a block is not pressure, it is that the block is already full of water, which the seasoning guide explains.

Electric presses exist. They apply automated pressure and remove the tightening step from a screw design. For the money they cost, the improvement over a twenty dollar spring press is small.

The purchase most people underrate is not a press at all. It is a wider pan, because crowding ruins more tofu than any missing tool. The pans guide covers that.

Durability and what fails

Presses fail in predictable places. Springs lose tension after a couple of years of heavy use and the press quietly stops applying much pressure, which people usually blame on the tofu. Threaded plastic posts strip if you overtighten them, and stainless posts do not. Silicone bands perish and go slack, though most are cheap to replace.

The plates themselves rarely fail. Cracks happen when someone forces a frozen or very cold block into a press, so thaw first.

If a press has been in use for a while and the tofu suddenly seems wetter, check the tension before checking anything else.

Getting the most out of one

Cut the block into slabs before pressing if you are in a hurry. More surface area drains faster, and half inch slabs press in about ten minutes rather than thirty.

Press in the refrigerator if you are going longer than an hour, or overnight. Tofu at room temperature for hours is not something to be casual about.

Save the pressed water for nothing. It has no culinary use and tastes of very little.

Consider freezing instead. A frozen and thawed block squeezes dry by hand with no press at all and comes out chewier and far better at taking marinade. The freezing guide covers the method, and for anyone who cooks tofu regularly it is a stronger upgrade than any press.
