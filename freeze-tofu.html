Freezing tofu

The step almost nobody hears about, and the one that does the most for texture. It costs nothing except a day of notice.

What happens inside the block

Tofu is a protein network holding a great deal of water. When you freeze it, that water turns to ice and expands, and the growing crystals push the protein structure aside to make room. The network is forced into denser walls around larger and larger pockets of ice.

When the block thaws, the ice melts and drains away, and what is left behind is the rearranged protein structure with open channels running through it. The tofu is chewier, noticeably more porous, and it looks slightly yellow or tan rather than white. That colour change is normal and is not a sign of spoilage.

Two things follow. The tofu squeezes almost dry by hand with no press required, and marinade travels into the block rather than sitting on the surface. Both of those are exactly the problems that make people give up on tofu.

The method

  1. Drain the block. Pressing first is optional and speeds up the freeze, but it is not required.
  2. Freeze it. In the unopened package works, though the water inside takes longer to freeze solid. Out of the package in a freezer bag or a sealed container is faster and takes less room.
  3. Freeze until solid all the way through. Overnight is reliable. A partly frozen block only gets the effect where the ice reached.
  4. Thaw. Overnight in the refrigerator gives the best texture. Several hours on the counter works. Under cool running water in a sealed bag takes about an hour when you are in a hurry.
  5. Squeeze. Hold the thawed block over the sink in both hands and press firmly. Water will pour out. Keep going until it stops running freely.
  6. Pat dry, cut, and cook as usual.

The squeeze is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters. A thawed block that has not been squeezed is wetter than an unpressed fresh one, because all that released water is sitting in the new channels waiting to flood your pan.

How long to freeze, and how many times

Overnight is enough. Longer does not improve the texture further, though a block can sit in the freezer for months without harm, and freezing is a genuinely good way to store tofu you were not going to use in time.

Freezing twice, thawing in between, produces an even more open and chewy texture. Some people prefer it. Others find it crosses over into dry and stringy. It is worth trying once so you know which camp you are in, but it is not the default.

The texture change is permanent and cannot be undone. If you freeze a block and decide you do not like the result, that block is not going back to being smooth.

What freezing is good for

Anything with a thick sauce that you want the tofu to absorb rather than wear. Barbecue tofu is the clearest example, since barbecue sauce is heavy and sits on the outside of a fresh block. On frozen and thawed tofu it soaks in and the flavour goes all the way through.

The same applies to any marinade you were disappointed by. If you have ever marinated tofu overnight and found the inside still tasted of nothing, freezing is the fix, not more time. The marinating guide explains why length of soak does so little on a fresh block.

Frozen and thawed tofu is also the right choice when you want a chewier, more substantial bite. It tears into irregular pieces that crisp beautifully at the ragged edges, which is a good trick for anyone who finds neat cubes of tofu off-putting to look at.

Skip freezing if: you want smooth, custardy or delicate tofu. Silken tofu in soup, tofu in a creamy sauce, mapo tofu, or anything where the pleasure is in the softness. Freezing permanently removes that quality. Also skip it if you are cooking tonight and did not plan ahead, because a partial freeze gives you the worst of both: some structural change and a wet, awkward block.

A practical routine

Tofu is usually cheaper in multipacks. Buy three or four blocks, put two straight into the freezer, and leave the rest in the refrigerator for fresh use. Move a frozen block into the refrigerator whenever you use one, so there is always a thawed block ready. That single habit removes the planning problem entirely.

Blocks freeze fine in their unopened packages, but they take up more room and take longer to thaw. Draining them and freezing them in a bag is faster on both ends and lets you stack them flat.

What to do next

Squeeze the thawed block dry, tear or cut it into pieces, and cook it hot. Because frozen tofu is drier to begin with, it crisps faster and browns more readily than fresh, so watch it more closely than you would otherwise. The crisping guide covers heat and timing, and barbecue tofu is the recipe that shows off what freezing does.

If you have not pressed a fresh block yet, start with the pressing guide. Freezing is the upgrade, but pressing is the foundation.
