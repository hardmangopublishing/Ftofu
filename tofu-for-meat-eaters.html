Tofu for people who eat meat

Tofu is not a meat replacement, and it works considerably better once you stop asking it to be one.

The comparison is the problem

Almost all tofu writing is aimed at people who have already stopped eating meat, which makes it close to useless for everyone else. It frames tofu as a substitute, compares it to chicken, loses that comparison, and then argues that you should not mind losing it.

Judged as chicken, tofu is a failure. It has no muscle fibre, so it cannot shred or pull apart along a grain. It has no connective tissue, so it does not become tender with time. It has no fat marbling, so it does not baste itself. It browns differently, because the browning reactions in meat rely on compounds tofu does not contain in the same quantities.

None of those are defects. They are just facts about a different food, and every one of them stops mattering the moment you are not scoring it against a cutlet.

What tofu is actually good at

It carries sauce better than almost anything. A crisp starched surface holds a glaze in a way that a smooth piece of chicken does not, and a frozen and thawed block absorbs flavour all the way through in a way meat cannot without brining.

It cooks in about twelve minutes. No resting, no temperature probe, no worrying about the middle. A block goes from package to plate in under forty minutes including pressing, and most of that is unattended.

It keeps. Unopened, it sits in the refrigerator for weeks and in the freezer for months. It is the protein that is still there when you have not shopped.

It costs less. Per gram of protein it undercuts almost every animal protein available, and consistently rather than only when something is on offer.

It is safe to undercook. Tofu is already edible from the package, so there is no version of this where getting the timing wrong is a food safety problem. That is a genuine relief for anyone who cooks anxiously.

A better mental model

Think of tofu as closer to halloumi, paneer or a firm mozzarella than to chicken. All of those are set curds, all of them hold their shape in a pan, all of them brown at the edges, and none of them are expected to taste of much on their own.

Nobody complains that paneer is bland, because nobody expected paneer to be the flavour. They expected it to be the substantial thing in the dish that carries the sauce, and that is exactly what tofu does.

The dishes that treat tofu this way have existed for centuries across East and Southeast Asia, where it sits alongside meat rather than instead of it. Mapo tofu contains pork. Plenty of traditional preparations do. The idea that tofu belongs exclusively to vegetarian cooking is a recent and largely Western framing.

Nobody here is asking you to give anything up

This site has no position on whether you should eat meat. Adding tofu to a rotation once a week is not a step toward anything, it is just a cheap fast protein that keeps well and takes sauce unusually nicely.

The recipes here are written on that basis. Several of them sit comfortably next to meat on the same table, and a few work well as a side dish rather than a centre.

If you eat tofu once a month and it is good every time, that is a better outcome than eating it weekly and resenting it.

What about tempeh and seitan

Both come up in the same conversation and neither is a version of tofu. Tempeh is whole soybeans fermented into a firm cake, so it keeps the fibre and has a nutty, faintly bitter flavour of its own. It does not absorb sauce the way tofu does, because it is already assertive, and it suits being sliced and pan-fried rather than cubed and glazed.

Seitan is wheat gluten, which puts it in a completely different category. It is chewy and genuinely meat-like in bite, and it is the right answer when you actually want a pulled or shredded texture that tofu cannot give you. It is also unsuitable for anyone avoiding gluten.

If tofu did not work for you, one of those two may. They are not competitors so much as three different foods that happen to share a shelf.

Where to start if you are sceptical

Cook basic crispy tofu with nothing on it but salt and eat a piece plain. It is four ingredients and about twelve minutes of cooking, and it tells you whether the technique works before you commit to a recipe with a shopping list.

If that piece is good, cook chili garlic tofu next, which is the version that changes the most minds, or buffalo tofu if the table is more of a wings crowd.

If that piece was not good, the problem is almost certainly pressing or crowding rather than tofu itself, and the diagnostic list works through the eleven usual causes in order.

Skip tofu entirely if: you have tried it cooked properly, several ways, and simply do not enjoy it. That is a legitimate outcome and this site is not going to argue with it. Nobody needs to like every food, and the goal here was only ever to make sure that a decision about tofu was made on a good plate of it rather than on a bad one.
