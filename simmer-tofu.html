Simmering and braising tofu

The route that skips pressing entirely. Heat, time and a sauce that reduces around the block drive flavour in where cold marinating cannot.

Why this page exists

Most of this site is about crisping, which is a Western adaptation of tofu cookery. It works, it converts skeptics, and it is genuinely useful if a bad texture is what put you off. It is also not how most tofu has been cooked, in most places, for most of the last two thousand years.

An enormous share of traditional tofu cooking presses nothing, freezes nothing, and adds no starch. The block goes into a sauce or a broth and cooks there. Presented honestly, that is not a shortcut or a lesser version. It is a different dish with a different pleasure, and for a lot of preparations it is the better one.

If you have read the four fixes and concluded that pressing is mandatory, this page is the correction.

Why it works when cold marinating does not

A fresh block is saturated with water, which is why a cold marinade sits on the surface and goes nowhere however long you leave it. That much is true and the seasoning guide covers it.

Heat changes the mechanism completely. Three things happen at once in a simmering pan.

  • The tofu gives up water. Proteins tighten as they heat and the block sheds liquid into the pan, which is pressing by other means and happens while you are cooking rather than beforehand.
  • The sauce concentrates. As liquid evaporates, what remains gets saltier and more intense, so the gradient driving flavour into the tofu keeps increasing.
  • Warm liquid moves faster. Diffusion into a porous solid is far quicker at simmering temperature than in a refrigerator.

The result is a block seasoned through rather than coated. Eight to fifteen minutes of gentle simmering does more for interior flavour than four hours of cold marinade on the same tofu.

Where this comes from

Simmered and braised tofu is central across East Asia and it is worth naming the traditions rather than presenting the method as a technique with no history.

Mapo tofu is Sichuan, and the softness of the unpressed cubes suspended in a chili bean sauce is the entire point of the dish. Dubu jorim is a Korean braise where slabs cook down in a soy, garlic and chili sauce until it clings. Chinese red-braised preparations simmer tofu with soy, sugar and aromatics until the liquid turns syrupy. Japanese simmered dishes cook tofu gently in dashi-based broth so it takes on the stock without collapsing.

This page is a general description of the method rather than a claim to any of those cuisines. If a specific dish interests you, look for a source rooted in it.

The method

  1. Choose the block. Firm or medium, straight from the package. Drain it and pat it dry. No pressing, no freezing.
  2. Cut generously. Half inch slabs or one inch cubes. Larger pieces survive stirring, and unpressed tofu is more fragile than the pressed kind.
  3. Sear briefly, or do not. Two or three minutes a side in a little oil gives a surface that holds up in the sauce and adds browning. Skipping it gives a softer, more delicate result. Both are legitimate.
  4. Add the sauce. Enough to come roughly halfway up the pieces. It should be saltier and more concentrated than you would want to drink, because it is about to be diluted by water leaving the tofu.
  5. Simmer gently. Eight to fifteen minutes at a bare bubble. A hard boil breaks the pieces apart and toughens the outside.
  6. Turn once, carefully. Use a spoon rather than tongs. Spoon sauce over the tops rather than stirring.
  7. Reduce at the end. Lift the tofu out if the sauce needs another few minutes, then return it. Never boil the tofu hard to thicken a sauce.
  8. Rest five minutes off the heat. This is the step that finishes the job. The tofu keeps drawing sauce in as it cools.

Neither column is the correct answer. They are different foods that happen to start from the same block.

A sauce that works

Soy sauce, water, a little sugar, garlic, ginger and chili in roughly a two to three ratio of soy to water, with the aromatics to taste. Cut a block into slabs, sear briefly if you like, add the sauce, and simmer twelve minutes turning once. Finish with sesame oil and green onion off the heat.

That is a whole dinner over rice and it needs no pressing, no starch, no freezer and no planning. If you have been putting off cooking tofu because the preparation sounded like a project, start here rather than with a crisped recipe.

Skip simmering if: you specifically want crunch. No amount of reduction produces a crisp edge, and a sauce that clings is not the same pleasure as a surface that snaps. If you are cooking for someone whose objection to tofu is mushiness, this method confirms their complaint rather than answering it. Give them chili garlic tofu instead, and cook this one for yourself.

An honest correction to the rest of this site

The four fixes are framed as solving tofu, and that framing is too strong. They solve one specific complaint, which is that tofu came out wet and bland when someone tried to cook it like a piece of chicken. That complaint is common enough in Western kitchens to be worth a site.

It is not the same as saying pressed and crisped tofu is correct tofu. Traditional preparations that do neither are not failing to press. They are cooking a different dish, usually one with more history behind it than anything here.

If this page is the one that gets you cooking tofu regularly, the rest of the site has done its job.
